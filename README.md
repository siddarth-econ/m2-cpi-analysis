# M2 Money Supply Growth and CPI Inflation: A Monetarist Lag Analysis (US- 2016–2026)

**Author:** Sid  
**Date:** July 2026  
**Subject:** IB Economics HL — Monetary Policy & Inflation

---

## Abstract

This paper examines the relationship between year-over-year M2 money supply growth and CPI inflation in the United States from 2016 to 2026- with a focus on the post-COVID monetary expansion. The data reveals a transmission lag broadly consistent with monetarist predictions- but the relationship is neither mechanical nor sufficient to explain inflation outcomes on its own.

---

## 1. Key Observations from the Data

### Peak Identification

- **Peak M2 growth:** ~27% YoY- occurring approximately in **February 2021**- driven by the cumulative effect of Fed asset purchases and fiscal stimulus (CARES Act- PPP- enhanced unemployment).
- **Peak CPI inflation:** ~9.1% YoY- occurring in **June 2022**- reflecting the pass-through of monetary expansion combined with persistent supply-chain disruptions and energy price shocks following Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
- **Lag between peaks:** Approximately **16 months**.

### The Contraction Phase

Equally revealing is the contraction side. M2 growth turned negative by late 2022- reaching roughly −4.5% YoY by mid-2023 - the first sustained contraction in modern US monetary history. CPI inflation responded with a decline from 9% to approximately 3% by late 2023- again with a lag of roughly 12–15 months. By 2025–26- M2 has recovered to ~5% YoY growth- and CPI has stabilised near 2.5–3%.

---

## 2. Consistency with Monetarist Theory

Milton Friedman's canonical claim is that monetary policy operates with "long and variable lags-" typically estimated at **12–18 months** for the transmission from money supply changes to consumer prices. The observed 16-month peak-to-peak lag in this dataset sits squarely within that range.

However- the *magnitude* of pass-through is far weaker than a crude quantity theory (MV = PY) would predict. M2 grew by ~27% at its peak- yet CPI peaked at only ~9%. This implies that a significant share of the monetary expansion was absorbed by:

1. **Velocity decline** — households saved stimulus payments rather than spending them immediately- compressing the velocity of money.
2. **Asset price inflation** — excess liquidity flowed into equities- housing- and crypto rather than consumer goods.
3. **Output recovery** — real GDP rebounded from the COVID trough- absorbing some of the nominal demand increase.

---

## 3. Correlation Analysis

A visual inspection of the two series reveals a moderate-to-strong positive correlation when the M2 series is lagged by 12–18 months. On the expansion side (2020–2022)- the co-movement is striking: the inflection points in CPI track the inflection points in M2 with a consistent offset. During the stable pre-COVID period (2016–2019)- the relationship is weaker — M2 grew at 4–7% with CPI anchored near 2%- suggesting that within a "normal" policy regime- other factors (expectations- supply conditions) dominate.

This points to a **non-linear relationship**: monetary expansion becomes inflationary primarily when it is large and rapid enough to overwhelm the economy's absorptive capacity- not as a continuous proportional mechanism.

---

## 4. Counterarguments and Limitations

### 4.1 The Eurozone and Japan Puzzle

Both the ECB and the Bank of Japan pursued aggressive monetary expansion during 2020–2021- with M2 growth rates elevated to levels comparable to the US. Yet Eurozone CPI peaked at ~10.6% (driven largely by energy- not domestic demand) and Japanese CPI peaked at only ~4.3%. If M2 growth were the primary causal driver- Japan's inflation should have been far higher. This suggests **fiscal policy transmission** (direct transfers to households in the US vs. corporate lending facilities in Japan) and **labour market structure** (US wage flexibility vs. Japanese wage rigidity) mediate the M2-to-CPI channel significantly.

### 4.2 Supply-Side Shocks

The CPI peak in June 2022 cannot be attributed to monetary factors alone. Energy prices surged following the Russia-Ukraine conflict- and semiconductor shortages- shipping bottlenecks- and food price spikes (Ukraine is a major grain exporter) all contributed. A decomposition of CPI into core and headline components shows that core inflation peaked later and lower- suggesting that at least 2–3 percentage points of the headline peak were supply-driven.

### 4.3 Expectations and Credibility

The relatively rapid disinflation from 9% to 3% — faster than most monetarist models predicted — may owe more to the Fed's credibility in signalling rate hikes than to the mechanical contraction of M2. Long-term inflation expectations (5Y5Y breakevens) remained anchored near 2.3% throughout- indicating that markets never priced in persistent inflation. This is difficult to reconcile with a purely monetarist framework.

---

## 5. Conclusion

The US data from 2016–2026 offers qualified support for the monetarist transmission mechanism: the lag structure is consistent with Friedman's 12–18-month estimate- and the directional co-movement during extreme episodes is clear. However- the magnitude mismatch- the cross-country variation- and the role of supply shocks and expectations all demonstrate that M2 growth is a **necessary but insufficient** condition for sustained inflation. The post-COVID episode is better understood as a confluence of monetary- fiscal- and supply-side factors rather than a vindication of any single theoretical framework.
